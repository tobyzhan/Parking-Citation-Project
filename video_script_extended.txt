"With our data cleaned and understood, we moved to clustering. We used K-means, 
an unsupervised learning algorithm that partitions data into K groups based on 
feature similarity. But here's the key: we didn't just use raw citations and 
average fines. That would miss important patterns.

We engineered richer features. Day-of-week distributions—which streets get more 
citations on weekdays versus weekends? Seasonal patterns—winter versus summer 
enforcement intensity. Violation-type proportions—what percentage of each street's 
citations involve meters versus signs versus street sweeping? This richer feature 
set captures how, when, and why enforcement happens.

[VISUAL: Silhouette score plot showing k=2 as optimal]

We tested solutions from k equals 2 to k equals 10 using silhouette scores—a metric 
that measures how well points cluster together. K equals 2 had the highest score. 
The data naturally wanted to split into TWO groups, not three.

[VISUAL: PCA scatter plot showing the two clusters]

When we visualized this with Principal Component Analysis—reducing our features 
to two dimensions for plotting—the split became obvious. One huge cluster 
representing 'typical' streets. One smaller cluster representing enforcement 
'hotspots.'

CLUSTER ZERO—'Typical Streets'—comprises the vast majority of San Diego streets. 
They have moderate citation volumes, showing a mix of violation types: street 
sweeping, posted sign violations, registration issues. These streets see routine, 
low-intensity enforcement spread evenly through the year.

CLUSTER ONE—'Enforcement Hotspots'—is a much smaller group concentrated in downtown

Notice what this means: the clustering works! It's not just finding the 'big'

NARRATOR:
"Now, here's the critical part: we didn't just accept the clustering results at 
face value. We validated them rigorously with statistical tests.

[VISUAL: Chi-square test graphic with contingency table]

First test: Chi-square test of independence. This tests whether violation types 
are independent of cluster membership. In plain English: Do different clusters 
really have different violation profiles? The result? Chi-square equals 12,847. 
P-value: practically zero. Highly significant. Violation types and clusters are 
NOT independent. They're strongly related.

[VISUAL: ANOVA test results]

Second test: We compared fine amounts across clusters. We used Kruskal-Wallis—the 
non-parametric alternative to ANOVA—because our fine distributions weren't 
perfectly normal. Result: H-statistic of 1,206. P-value: less than 0.001. 
Significant. Average fines DO differ between clusters.

The statistical evidence was overwhelming. Our clusters weren't artifacts of the 
algorithm. They reflect real, measurable differences in how enforcement works 
across different parts of San Diego."
